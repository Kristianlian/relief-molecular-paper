Introduction

Skeletal muscle mass and strength decline with age, a process that advances from approximately 50 years of age, accelerates after 70 and, if left unchecked, may progress to sarcopenia1,2. Low muscle mass and strength are associated with disease, disability and early all-cause mortality, and regular resistance training (RT) is the most effective strategy to prevent or counteract this decline1,3,4. Older adults are expected to gain similar benefits from RT as the young5,6, provided the anabolic stimulus is sufficient5–7, of which training volume is a principal determinant8.

Whether the anabolic stimulus is sufficient may in part depend on how responsive aging skeletal muscle is to it. Earlier findings suggest that older adults have a less responsive skeletal muscle mass compared to young, with a lower muscle protein synthesis (MPS) response to a single session of RT or protein ingestion2,9,10. However, baseline levels of MPS do not differ by age, and the activity in MPS-influencing proteins (e.g. mTOR) is elevated in fasted muscle from older adults compared to the young2,11,12. The increased baseline mTOR activity could represent a compensation to increased relative muscle loading due to age-related loss of muscle mass, or dysregulation of the mTOR complex2. Further, likely as a consequence of altered mTOR regulation, ribosomal gene transcription and ribosome biogenesis is reduced in older adults2,11,13. The increase in ribosomal content following onset of RT is believed to provide support for skeletal muscle fibre hypertrophy, through increased translational capacity yielding a higher baseline MPS14,15. Indeed, regardless of age, individuals who respond to RT exhibit an accumulation in markers of ribosome biogenesis (e.g., total RNA and/or rRNA), compared to those who do not experience benefits of RT16–18.

Given the dose-response relationship between training volume and gains in muscle mass and strength observed in younger adults8, and recent findings that older “non-responders” can achieve improved hypertrophy with higher training volume19, increasing training volume may represent a strategy to enhance the anabolic stimulus and thereby support the ribosome biogenesis response necessary for adaptation. Therefore, the present study aimed to determine whether markers of ribosome biogenesis (total RNA, UBF, c-Myc) and the transcriptome respond differently to low- and moderate-volume RT in young and older adults.
