Introduction

##Paragraph: Resistance training is a good way to improve muscle mass and strength, but responses to RT seem highly individual, especially with age.

Resistance training (RT) is recommended as the best non-pharmacological strategy to ameliorate age-related declines in physical functionality due to losses of skeletal muscle mass and -strength1, and such benefits are expected irrespective of age at onset of structured and repeated RT1,2. However, previous studies find variations in how individuals respond to RT, regardless of age and sex8. Since lower skeletal muscle mass and -strength are associated with higher risks of mortality9,10, not benefiting from a period of RT is especially troubling for the elderly. While the underlying mechanisms of individual variations to RT are not fully elucidated, differential manifestations of aging make the older population quite more heterogeneous than the young2,11. This heterogeneity in physical function is reflected by, perhaps most notably, the different presence of pathophysiological conditions such as insulin resistance, sarcopenia, and anabolic resistance1,2,11.

##Paragraph: The skeletal muscle of young may be better suited to respond to resistance training.

The relationship between insulin resistance and sarcopenia has been a hot topic, and which one leads to the other has yet to be concluded2,12. Nevertheless, lean body mass and thigh skeletal muscle cross-sectional area is maintained despite the presence of insulin resistance and inflammation2,13,14, while sarcopenia is known as age-related progressive loss of muscle mass and -strength1,2. Furthermore, a lower response in muscle protein synthesis (MPS) after RT has been observed in the old compared to young individuals, described as anabolic resistance15,16. Whether anabolic resistance is a contributor to or a consequence of sarcopenia is uncertain, but it does indicate a less responsive skeletal muscle mass in the elderly15,16. More precisely, studeis suggest that baseline levels of muscle protein synthesis per se do not differ with age, rather, the proteins influencing MPS (e.g. mTOR) are elevated in fasted muscle from the elderly compared to young2,11,17. Such a discrepancy in MPS is proposed to represent either a compensation to increased muscle loading due to decreased muscle mass with age, or dysregulation of the mTOR complex2. In a rodent synergist ablation model, it was evident that the elderly mice had lower transcription of ribosomal genes compared with young, and returned faster to baseline in the old mice2,18. Transcription of ribosomal genes occur downstream of mTOR, and can therefore reflect some degree of mTOR dysregulation in elderly mice2,18.

##Paragraph: Ribosome biogenesis is a common denominator in those who do not experience expected benefits of RT, across age. (either include in third paragraph or make a fourth)

A reduced transcription of ribosomal genes may however occur across age, since a blunted accumulation of total RNA has been observed in both young and old, coinciding with lesser benefits of RT6,19,20. Ribosomes are the protein factories of the cell, and the increase in ribosomal content (translational capacity) following onset of RT is believed to provide support for skeletal muscle fibre hypertrophy21,22. Results from our laboratory, as well as others, suggests that the accumulation of total RNA (a proxy marker of ribosome biogenesis) peaks between 2-4 weeks after onset of RT (depending on training volume)19,20,23, remain elevated throughout 7 weeks of RT24–26, and returns to baseline after 8 weeks25,27,28. This increase in total RNA shortly following onset of RT is observed both in untrained6,19,20,24 and moderately trained23,25 individuals, alongside increases in muscle mass and/or -strength19,23. Regardless of age, individuals who respond to RT exhibit an accumulation in the markers of ribosome biogenesis (e.g., total RNA and rRNA), compared to those who do not experience benefits of RT6,19,20. . The findings from Hammarstrom et al.19 and Stec et al.20 along with research from Figueiredo et al.21,22 suggests that ribosome biogenesis is indeed a prerequisite for RT-induced adaptations, increasing the translational capacity of the exercised skeletal muscle to support a higher MPS and thus hypertrophy. Though occuring regardless of age, the variation in ribosome biogenesis is proposed to be larger or more impactful among the elderly, and recent investigations find an impaired ribosome biogenesis in aging skeletal muscle to be a likely contributor to anabolic resistance11,29. However, in rodents and human cell cultures, application of an exercise stimulus shifts aging muscle towards a younger profile30, potentially representing a treatment to the age-related impairment of ribosome biogenesis and subsequently anabolic resistance.

##Paragraph: Increased training volume is expected to enhance the adaptations to RT, and elderly may need a higher training volume than young

The dose-response relationship between resistance training volume and skeletal muscle hypertrophy has previously been described31,32, where a higher training volume elicits a stronger hypertrophic signal in the skeletal muscle. Therefore, an increased RT volume should in theory provide sufficient hypertrophic stimulus to the less responsive skeletal muscle of the elderly and ensure prevention or treatment of age-related conditions such as sarcopenia..

it is logical that a higher RT volume may be necessary to elicit responses in the elderly compared to the young

Currently, the literature suggests that the ability to synthesise ribosomes is affected by both resistance training19,21,33 and age whereas RT should initiate ribosome biogenesis, a response that may be reduced in the elderly. Therefore, it is necessary to determine how to optimise RT to ensure such initation of ribosome biogenesis.
